# Audience Guide Template

An audience guide describes the people a piece is written for: their situation, what they already know, what they want, and what makes them skeptical. A voice guide controls how the writing sounds. An audience guide controls what it explains, which examples it picks, and what evidence it needs.

Adapted from the AUDIENCE.md template in Every's compound-writing project (MIT license; see README credits), with a shared-vocabulary section added.

## Rules for writing one

- **Evidence or blank.** Every observation names its basis. Leave a section open rather than invent a persona to fill it.
- **Label the basis.** Use one of: `documented` (reader feedback, transcripts, surveys, sales notes), `positioning` (how we choose to describe the audience; this is intent, not proof of behavior), `writer instruction` (the owner said so), or `inference` (our reading of the evidence; say what it rests on).
- **Keep reader language.** When a reader's own words exist, quote them. Exact language beats our paraphrase.
- **The assignment wins.** If a piece names a narrower or different reader, that reader overrides this guide.
- **Never personal data.** Describe situations and roles, never named people or identifiable organizations.

Save finished guides as `<audience-name>.md` in one of the folders the rewriter searches (see SKILL.md, Audience Check).

## Who they are and what they face

A short paragraph in plain prose: the reader's situation, why this subject reaches them, and the tension or opportunity in front of them. Describe situations, not just titles.

- Who encounters this writing, and where (inbox, LinkedIn feed, a meeting pre-read)?
- What are they trying to get done or figure out?
- Which differences between readers change the explanation or the examples?

## What they know, and what they don't

- What can the writing safely assume?
- What needs explaining every time?
- What must never be assumed (budget, technical staff, time, prior AI experience)?

### Shared vocabulary

Terms this reader already uses, terms they misread, and terms to avoid or define on first use. This is the fastest cure for the curse of knowledge: the writer's shorthand is often the reader's fog.

## What they want, and what would help

A **want** is the outcome the reader is after. A **help** is the understanding or support that gets them there. Label each row; an inferred need is a guess about someone else's motives, so mark it as one.

## Where they are on the path (optional)

Use only when it changes a writing decision. Readers enter at different points, and curiosity alone is a fine reason to read. Leave unsupported rows open.

## What makes them skeptical, and what earns trust

- What has made these readers doubtful, bored, or shut out before?
- What evidence actually moves them?
- Which observations hold only for a subgroup?

## How the rewriter uses this guide

- **Strategist:** checks assumed knowledge against "What they know," and picks examples from the reader's situations.
- **Nudge mode and the cold reader:** reads the draft as this person, using the vocabulary table and skepticism notes.
- **Editor:** confirms the piece offers something from the "honestly offer" column and claims nothing the evidence table can't support.

The audience guide never adds facts to a draft. It shapes what to explain and what to prove; the facts still come from the source.
