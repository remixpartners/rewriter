# Critique Modes

Five ways to give feedback on a draft, matched to how finished the draft is. The mode names (Inspire, Provoke, Clarify, Nudge, Polish) come from the Modes of Critique framework developed at IDEO Chicago around 2016. The descriptions, prompts, and procedures below are this skill's own.

The point of naming the mode: the most common feedback failure is the wrong kind at the wrong time. Line edits on a draft whose argument isn't settled waste everyone's effort. "What if you started over?" on a piece due in an hour is demoralizing. Agreeing on the mode first makes the feedback usable.

## The spectrum (say this to the user every time you offer a mode)

Never assume the user knows these terms. Whenever you name or offer a mode, explain the whole spectrum in a few lines so they can see where their draft sits. Use this, adapted to the draft in front of you:

> Feedback works best when it matches how finished the draft is. Think of it as a line from wide open to nearly done:
>
> 1. **Inspire** (wide open): you have raw material but no angle. I bring possible directions, examples, and ideas to borrow.
> 2. **Provoke** (open): you have an angle, but it feels safe or flat. I push with "what if" challenges.
> 3. **Clarify** (narrowing): there's good material pulling in too many directions. I help you find the one core idea.
> 4. **Nudge** (focused): the idea is set. I test it the way your reader will and suggest specific improvements.
> 5. **Polish** (nearly done): I tighten the writing itself without changing what it says.
>
> The first two open up options; the next two narrow them; Polish finishes. For this draft I'd suggest **[mode]**, because [one specific reason from the draft]. Want that, or a different one?

Keep the explanation short. Once the user has seen it in a session, a one-line reminder is enough ("We're in Clarify, the narrowing stage").

## Choosing a mode

| Signal in the draft or request | Suggest |
|---|---|
| Bullet fragments, a transcript, "I have some thoughts about...", no stated point | Inspire |
| A clear point that sounds like what everyone already says; "this feels boring"; "make it land harder" | Provoke |
| Several competing points; the opening and ending argue different things; "I don't know what this is about anymore" | Clarify |
| One clear point, full draft, worry about a specific reader or a specific section; "will this convince them?" | Nudge |
| Finished argument, the user wants it cleaner, shorter, or in a voice; "rewrite", "polish", "tighten", "clean up" | Polish |

When signals conflict, suggest the earlier (more open) mode and say why. The user always picks.

## Rules for every mode

- **The thesis belongs to the writer.** No mode changes the main argument without asking. A stronger idea found in the draft is offered as an option, never installed.
- **Every mode ends at a decision point.** Inspire, Provoke, Clarify, and Nudge deliver critique and stop. Nothing gets rewritten until the user decides. Polish delivers a rewrite plus a change list they can reverse.
- **Specific, located, quoted.** Point at the exact sentence. "The opening is slow" is not feedback; "the first two paragraphs are background; your point arrives in paragraph three: '...'" is.
- **Use the audience.** If an audience guide is active (see SKILL.md, Audience Check), every mode judges against that reader, not a generic one.

## 1. Inspire

**When:** Raw material, no angle yet. The writer needs options, not edits.

**What you do:**
- Read the material and name what's actually in it: the two or three ideas with real energy, and any concrete story, number, or example worth building on.
- Offer three to five distinct directions the piece could take. Each one gets a one-line premise, the material from the source that would carry it, and the reader it would serve best.
- Where useful, suggest borrowings: a structure from a well-known piece, an analogy from another field, an example type that would make the idea concrete. Label these as borrowings, never as facts about the writer's situation.

**Prompt shape:** "Have you considered..." "Another way in would be..."

**Deliver:** The directions as a numbered list. No rewrite.

**Decision point:** "Which direction should we develop? Pick a number, combine two, or tell me what's missing."

**Never:** invent facts, results, or stories to make a direction look better.

## 2. Provoke

**When:** The angle exists, but it plays it safe. The draft sounds like conventional wisdom, or the writer suspects it won't stick.

**What you do:**
- Find the assumption the draft rests on and flip it. Add or remove a constraint: "What if this had to be three sentences?" "What if the reader disagreed with you?" "What if you opened with the failure instead of the win?"
- Read as a hostile but fair critic and name the strongest objection a smart skeptic would raise. One sharp objection beats five mild ones.
- Look for the part of the material that breaks a pattern (Heath: common sense is the enemy of sticky writing). That's often the real piece.

**Prompt shape:** "What if..." "A skeptic would say..."

**Deliver:** Three to five provocations, numbered, each with the passage it targets.

**Decision point:** "Do any of these change what you want to say? Pick one to pursue, or keep your angle and we move to Clarify."

**Never:** treat a provocation as a recommendation. It's a test; the writer may keep their angle.

## 3. Clarify

**When:** There's good material pulling in several directions. The writer needs the one core idea.

**What you do:**

1. **The one-sentence test.** Write the piece's core idea as one sentence. If you can't without joining two unrelated claims with "and," the piece has two centers. Say so and offer both.
2. **Three versions of the point.** Compare:
   - **Promised point:** what the headline, subject line, or opening tells the reader the piece is about.
   - **Delivered point:** what the full piece actually spends its words supporting.
   - **Hidden point:** a stronger idea buried later, if one exists. It must be more consequential, not just more vivid.
3. **Placement check.** Is the delivered point where this format needs it? An email, memo, or recommendation should state it immediately. An essay or post can open with a hook or scene, but the point must arrive before curiosity turns to confusion. Stop reading where a busy reader would (subject line plus first sentence for email; first two lines for LinkedIn) and state what they'd walk away believing.
4. **The 20-second test.** Could the writer explain the piece aloud to a peer in 20 seconds? Draft that version. If it's better than the opening, the opening is wrong.
5. **Verdict.** One of: *Up front* (the right point is where it belongs), *Delayed but earned* (the setup makes the point land harder), *Buried* (unneeded material delays it), *Wrong lead* (the opening foregrounds a lesser idea), or *Split* (two or more ideas compete).

**Deliver:**

```
Core idea (one sentence): ...
Promised point: ...    Delivered point: ...    Hidden point: ... (if any)
Verdict: ...
20-second version: ...
Smallest fix: [promote a sentence, cut a warm-up, reorder two paragraphs; quote the location]
```

**Decision point:** "Is this the sentence you want the reader to walk away with? Confirm it or edit it." The confirmed sentence becomes the Strategist's Commander's Intent if the user then asks for a rewrite.

**Never:** rebuild the piece around the hidden point without the writer's yes.

## 4. Nudge

**When:** The idea is set and the draft is whole. The writer wants rigor on one aspect, or wants to know how it will land.

**What you do:** "Yes, and." Accept the piece's intent and make it stronger. Ask which aspect matters most if the user hasn't said; otherwise run these three checks:

1. **Cold read.** Read in order as the target reader encountering this for the first time, with none of the writer's background. At each point of friction, quote the passage and name the reaction:
   - *I don't follow* (a term, reference, or step is unclear)
   - *I'm missing context* (setup the piece assumes but never gives)
   - *I don't believe it yet* (the claim asks for more trust than it has earned)
   - *Why am I reading this?* (stakes or relevance are unclear)
   - *I feel talked down to, or shut out* (tone, jargon, presumption)
   - *I'm drifting* (repetitive, abstract, slow)
   - *That's not what you promised* (the piece sets up something and abandons it)

   Rate each: stops the read, causes a stumble, or worth noticing. Confusion created early isn't erased by an explanation later.
2. **Objections.** Read as a smart outsider who has never heard of the writer and extends no goodwill. Find claims that only work if you already trust the source, in-group shorthand, and confident tone standing in for evidence. Name each objection by type (factual, "not my experience," "doesn't apply to me," "I don't see how that follows," "what's the downside," "there's an easier way") and rank by how likely it is to lose the reader.
3. **Evidence check.** For each load-bearing claim: is it supported in the draft? Is the support specific? Does the certainty in the prose match the certainty in the source?

**Deliver:** A numbered list of nudges, most important first. Each one: the quoted passage, what's wrong for this reader, and a concrete "yes, and" suggestion. Cap at about ten; group minor items.

**Decision point:** "Which nudges should I apply? Reply with numbers (e.g. 1, 3, 4), 'all', or 'all but 2'." Then apply only those, as a Polish-intensity rewrite of the affected passages, and leave everything else untouched.

**Never:** sneak in nudges the user didn't pick.

## 5. Polish

**When:** The argument is final. The writer wants better sentences, a tighter piece, or a specific voice.

**What you do:** Run the rewriter pipeline (SKILL.md Steps 2 through 4) at the tier chosen in Step 0. In Polish, the structure and the point stay put unless intensity is 4 to 5.

**Deliver:** The rewritten piece, followed by a short numbered change list of the meaningful edits (not every comma):

```
Changes
1. Cut the opening paragraph (background the reader doesn't need yet).
2. "leverage our capabilities" -> "use what we built".
3. Moved the pricing sentence to the end of paragraph 2.
...
Reply "revert 2" or "revert 1, 3" to undo any of these.
```

**Decision point:** The user reverts by number, or accepts.

**Never:** list changes that didn't happen, or omit a change that altered meaning.

---

## After the decision: moving along the spectrum

Modes are a path, not a menu you pick from once. After each decision point, suggest the natural next mode: Inspire leads to Clarify (narrow the chosen direction), Provoke leads to Clarify or Nudge, Clarify leads to Nudge or Polish, Nudge leads to Polish. Ask; don't assume.

## Learning from decisions (propose, never write)

If the user rejects the same kind of suggestion twice in a session (for example, reverting every cut of their rhetorical questions), propose a one-line rule for their voice guide or audience guide: "You've kept your rhetorical questions both times. Add 'rhetorical questions at section turns are intentional' to your voice guide?" Write nothing until they say yes. If the guide lives in a git repository, the change goes through that repository's normal review process.
